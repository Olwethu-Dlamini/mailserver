Part 03 · The theory

DNS and Trust: What a Receiving Server Checks

Part 02 ended on an uncomfortable fact: SMTP will let anyone type any From: address. Everything that makes email trustworthy today was bolted on afterwards, and almost all of it lives in DNS. This part walks the checks a receiving server runs on my mail, in the order it runs them, so the DNS records in Part 08 are not a ritual.

Reading time about 20 minutes. Longest theory part; the build pages lean on it heavily.

1. Why trust lives in DNS

When Gmail's server receives a connection claiming to carry mail from 22112002.xyz, it has two facts it cannot fake: the IP address that connected, and the bytes of the message. Everything else in the conversation is text the sender typed. The question is how Gmail can turn those two facts into "yes, the owner of 22112002.xyz really sent this".

The answer is that only the owner of a domain can publish records under it. So each authentication scheme has the domain owner publish a statement in DNS ("these IPs may send for me", "here is my public key", "here is what to do if the checks fail"), and the receiver reads that statement and compares it to the two facts it has. DNS becomes the place where my domain speaks for itself.

The order below is roughly the order a receiving MTA performs the checks, from cheapest to most expensive, and it matters: a sender that fails the early checks is often refused before the message body is even sent.

2. Forward DNS: MX, A, and the Cloudflare trap

Before any of the trust checks, there is the plumbing. For mail to reach me, a sender resolves my MX record to a hostname and that hostname to an IP:

22112002.xyz.          MX    10 mail.22112002.xyz.
mail.22112002.xyz.     A     <the VPS's public IPv4>

Two rules from the specifications: the MX target must be a hostname, not an IP literal, and that hostname must have an A/AAAA record directly, not be a CNAME to something else. Both are enforced loosely by real servers, but breaking them is a source of "works with some senders, not others" bugs I would rather never debug.

The Cloudflare trap

On Cloudflare, every A record has an orange-cloud/grey-cloud toggle. Orange means Cloudflare's HTTP proxy sits in front and the record resolves to Cloudflare's IPs. That is what makes the websites work. For mail.22112002.xyz it would be fatal: a sender would resolve the name to a Cloudflare IP, connect to port 25 there, and get nothing, because Cloudflare's proxy only speaks HTTP. The mail host's A record must be grey cloud, "DNS only". The Cloudflare dashboard actually warns when an MX points at a proxied name, which is how I first learned this.

The web side needs nothing new. 22112002.xyz keeps its tunnel CNAME, the websites keep working, and webmail.22112002.xyz is just one more tunnel hostname. Only the one name that mail servers connect to has to be a plain, honest A record pointing at a machine that answers on 25.

3. Reverse DNS: PTR and forward confirmation

Forward DNS turns a name into an IP. Reverse DNS turns an IP into a name, through a PTR record in a special zone under in-addr.arpa. The receiving server looks up the PTR of the connecting IP and then, crucially, resolves that name forward again and checks it comes back to the same IP. This round trip is called forward-confirmed reverse DNS (FCrDNS), and large receivers treat failing it as a strong spam signal or a reason to refuse outright.

<VPS IP>             PTR   mail.22112002.xyz.      ← set at the VPS provider
mail.22112002.xyz.    A     <VPS IP>               ← set at Cloudflare; must match

The thing that took me a while to accept: I cannot set a PTR record in Cloudflare or anywhere else I control, because PTR records live in the zone owned by whoever owns the IP block. For a VPS that is the hosting provider, which exposes a "reverse DNS" field in its control panel. For my home connection it is my ISP, who will not. This single fact is a complete, independent reason the outbound path has to leave from the VPS, even if nothing in Part 01 applied.

It also means the same hostname should be used in three places: the PTR, the A record, and the name the relay announces in its EHLO greeting. When all three agree, check 1 in the diagram passes cleanly.

4. SPF: which IPs may send for my domain

Sender Policy Framework is the oldest and simplest of the three authentication schemes. The domain owner publishes a TXT record listing the IPs allowed to send mail whose envelope sender (the MAIL FROM from Part 02) is at that domain. The receiver takes the connecting IP and asks: is it on the list?

22112002.xyz.    TXT   "v=spf1 mx -all"

Reading that: version 1; any IP that my MX records resolve to is allowed (mx); nobody else is, and treat them as a hard fail (-all). Because my MX is mail.22112002.xyz and that resolves to the VPS, the VPS is authorised with no IP written into the record, so if the VPS ever changes I update one A record and SPF follows. Other mechanisms I read about and did not need:

  • ip4:203.0.113.5 or ip4:203.0.113.0/24: an explicit address or range.
  • include:_spf.google.com: "also allow whatever that other domain's SPF allows". Needed when a third party (a newsletter service, a transactional relay) sends as you. Each include costs DNS lookups and there is a hard limit of ten per evaluation; exceeding it is a permanent error.
  • a: the domain's own A record. Wrong for me, because that is a Cloudflare proxy IP.
  • ~all instead of -all: "softfail", meaning "probably not legitimate, but do not refuse on SPF alone". Common advice is to start here. With DMARC in place (below), -all is fine and clearer about intent.

What SPF checks and what it misses. It binds the connecting IP to the envelope sender domain. It says nothing about the From: header a human sees, so on its own it does not stop a forger who uses their own envelope domain and my address in From:. And it breaks under forwarding: if my friend has their Gmail forward to another account, that final receiver sees Gmail's IP sending a message with my envelope domain, which my record does not allow. That weakness is the entire reason DKIM exists.

5. DKIM: a signature that travels with the message

DomainKeys Identified Mail takes a different approach: instead of vouching for an IP, the sending server signs the message with a private key, and publishes the matching public key in DNS. Any receiver, at any later hop, can fetch the key and verify the signature. Forwarding does not break it, because the signature is inside the message.

The signing server adds a header like this (shortened):

DKIM-Signature: v=1; a=rsa-sha256; d=22112002.xyz; s=202610;
   h=from:to:subject:date:message-id; bh=<hash of the body>;
   b=<signature over the listed headers and bh>

Two fields matter for the receiver: d=, the signing domain, and s=, the selector. It builds the name <selector>._domainkey.<domain>, fetches the TXT record there, and gets the public key:

202610._domainkey.22112002.xyz.   TXT   "v=DKIM1; k=rsa; p=MIIBIjANBgkq..."

The selector exists so a domain can have several keys at once. That is what makes rotation possible: publish a new key under a new selector, start signing with it, and after old messages in transit have all been verified, delete the old selector's record. Rotating is good hygiene (a leaked key is otherwise valid forever) and tedious by hand, which is why I was glad to find that Stalwart manages DKIM key rotation itself and, with automatic DNS management, publishes the new selector's record to Cloudflare for me. Part 04 covers that; the point here is that the mechanism in DNS is just "one TXT record per selector".

What DKIM checks and what it misses. It binds the message content to the d= domain: whoever holds that domain's private key produced these exact headers and body. It says nothing about which IP sent it, and, like SPF, nothing about the From: header, because d= can be any domain the signer controls. A spammer can DKIM-sign with their own domain and still put mine in From:. Closing that last gap is DMARC's job.

6. DMARC: tying both to the From: header

DMARC does three things that neither SPF nor DKIM does alone. It looks at the domain in the From: header, the one a human sees. It requires that at least one of SPF or DKIM passed and that the domain it passed for is the same as (or a subdomain of) that From: domain. This is called alignment. And it tells the receiver what to do when neither aligns, plus where to send reports about it.

_dmarc.22112002.xyz.   TXT   "v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc@22112002.xyz; adkim=s; aspf=s"
  • p= is the policy for mail that fails: none (deliver normally, just report), quarantine (spam folder), reject (refuse at SMTP time).
  • rua= is where receivers send aggregate reports: daily XML files summarising, per sending IP, how many messages they saw claiming my domain and whether SPF and DKIM aligned. They are unreadable raw and perfectly readable through any free DMARC report viewer. Stalwart also parses and displays them under Reports.
  • adkim=s / aspf=s ask for strict alignment, exact domain match, instead of the relaxed default that accepts subdomains. For a single-domain setup, strict costs nothing.

The rollout that does not lock me out of my own mail

Every guide I read agrees on the sequence, and it is the one I am following in Part 08:

  1. Publish with p=none and a rua= address. Nothing changes for receivers; I start getting reports.
  2. Send mail normally for a couple of weeks and read the reports. Every legitimate source of mail from my domain (the server itself, the LMS sending password resets, anything else) should show as aligned. Anything that is not, I fix: usually a service that needs an include: in SPF or a DKIM key of its own.
  3. Move to p=quarantine. Forged mail now lands in spam folders.
  4. Move to p=reject. Forged mail is refused at the door. This is also the setting that lets receivers trust my domain the most.
Why not reject from day one

Because the LMS on lms.22112002.xyz sends mail too, through PHP, and I have not yet checked what envelope and From: it uses. If I jump to reject before routing it through Stalwart, its password-reset emails vanish and I will not know until a user tells me. The reports exist to find exactly this.

7. Transport security: MTA-STS, TLS-RPT and DANE

The checks so far are about who sent a message. These are about whether the connection carrying it was encrypted. SMTP's STARTTLS is opportunistic: if a middlebox strips the STARTTLS line from the server's greeting, the sender quietly continues in plain text. Two schemes let a domain owner say "do not accept that".

MTA-STS (Mail Transfer Agent Strict Transport Security) works over HTTPS, so it needs no DNSSEC. The domain publishes a tiny TXT record saying "I have a policy, version id", and serves the policy itself as a text file at a fixed HTTPS URL:

_mta-sts.22112002.xyz.   TXT   "v=STSv1; id=20261008"

https://mta-sts.22112002.xyz/.well-known/mta-sts.txt :
version: STSv1
mode: testing
mx: mail.22112002.xyz
max_age: 86400

A sending server that supports MTA-STS fetches that file, caches it for max_age seconds, and from then on refuses to deliver to me unless the connection is TLS with a valid certificate for one of the listed MX hosts. mode: testing means "report failures but still deliver"; enforce means refuse. Same pattern as DMARC: test first.

The nice consequence for my setup is that mta-sts.22112002.xyz is an ordinary HTTPS site, so it goes through the Cloudflare Tunnel like everything else, with Caddy proxying to Stalwart's HTTP listener, which generates the policy file from its domain settings. The one piece of mail infrastructure that is HTTP gets to use the tunnel.

TLS-RPT is the reporting companion: a TXT record at _smtp._tls naming an address where senders email a daily summary of TLS failures they had connecting to me.

_smtp._tls.22112002.xyz.   TXT   "v=TLSRPTv1; rua=mailto:tlsrpt@22112002.xyz"

DANE solves the same problem through DNS instead of HTTPS: a TLSA record at _25._tcp.mail.22112002.xyz publishes a hash of the certificate (or its issuer), and senders verify against it. It is the stricter design, but it only works if the DNS answers are themselves signed with DNSSEC, and the certificate hash has to be updated every time Let's Encrypt renews. Cloudflare supports DNSSEC with one click, and Stalwart can generate the TLSA records, so this is possible later. I am marking it optional: MTA-STS gets most of the benefit with none of the renewal coupling.

8. Records for my own clients: autoconfig and autodiscover

None of this is about trust, but it lives in the same zone. When someone types me@22112002.xyz into Thunderbird or an iPhone, the client tries to find the server settings on its own. Thunderbird fetches https://autoconfig.22112002.xyz/mail/config-v1.1.xml; Outlook fetches https://autodiscover.22112002.xyz/autodiscover/autodiscover.xml; others look up SRV records:

_imaps._tcp.22112002.xyz.        SRV   0 1 993 mail.22112002.xyz.
_submissions._tcp.22112002.xyz.  SRV   0 1 465 mail.22112002.xyz.
autoconfig.22112002.xyz.         CNAME mail.22112002.xyz.    (or via the tunnel)
autodiscover.22112002.xyz.       CNAME mail.22112002.xyz.    (or via the tunnel)

Stalwart serves the XML files from its HTTP listener, and like MTA-STS they can go through the tunnel. The payoff is small but real: adding the account on a phone becomes "type address and password".

9. Blocklists: the check I cannot influence with records

Check 2 in the diagram is the DNS-based blocklist lookup. The receiver reverses the connecting IP's octets, appends a list's zone (for example zen.spamhaus.org), and does an A lookup. An answer means "listed", and the specific answer encodes why. Spamhaus ZEN bundles several lists; the ones that matter for me:

  • PBL, the policy list from Part 01: ranges that ISPs have declared are end-user space. My home IP is on it by design and nothing I publish changes that. The VPS's IP must not be.
  • SBL and XBL: known spam sources and compromised machines. A fresh VPS IP can inherit a listing from its previous tenant, which is one reason to check the IP against ZEN before building anything on it. Part 08 does this first.

Stalwart runs these same lookups on inbound connections as part of its spam filter, which is why Part 05 goes to some trouble to make sure Stalwart sees the real connecting IP through the relay rather than the relay's own.

Glossary for this part

Grey cloud / orange cloud
Cloudflare's per-record switch. Orange proxies HTTP through Cloudflare's IPs; grey publishes the real IP. Mail hosts must be grey.
PTR / FCrDNS
Reverse DNS for an IP, and the check that the resulting name resolves forward to the same IP. Set by the IP's owner, which is why it must be the VPS.
SPF
A TXT record listing which IPs may send mail whose envelope sender is at the domain. Checked against the connecting IP. Breaks under forwarding.
DKIM / selector
A signature over the message, verified with a public key published at selector._domainkey.domain. Selectors allow several keys and so rotation.
DMARC / alignment
A policy at _dmarc.domain requiring that SPF or DKIM passed for the domain in the From: header, and saying what to do otherwise.
p=none / quarantine / reject
DMARC's three answers to "what if it fails": deliver and report, spam-folder it, refuse it.
Aggregate report (rua)
Daily XML sent by receivers summarising which IPs sent as your domain and whether they aligned. The feedback loop for rolling out DMARC.
MTA-STS / TLS-RPT
An HTTPS-published policy that tells senders to require TLS and a valid certificate when delivering to you, and the record naming where they report failures.
DANE / TLSA
The DNS-based equivalent: a certificate hash in DNS, trusted because DNSSEC signs it.
DNSBL
A blocklist queried over DNS by reversing the IP. Spamhaus ZEN is the usual one; its PBL component lists residential ranges.
Autoconfig / autodiscover
Files and SRV records that let a mail client find server settings from just an email address.
